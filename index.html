SkateNav: Learning Unified Adaptive Skating and Local Navigation for Passive-Wheel Quadrupedal Robots

A single policy decides steering, intermittent leg-driven propulsion, and the temporal allocation between pushing and gliding.

Author One1, Author Two1, Author Three1, Hyun Myung1

1 Urban Robotics Lab, KAIST

Abstract

Where to arrive and by when — not how fast

The command that lets a passive-wheel robot glide

Passive-wheel quadrupedal robots generate propulsion by pushing the ground with their legs and glide on the resulting momentum, covering distance without continuous actuation. However, navigating such a robot requires steering to be coordinated with intermittent propulsion, and the temporal allocation between pushing and gliding to follow the navigation context.

Recent reinforcement learning-based skating controllers have improved the speed and robustness of passive-wheel locomotion. However, they track a commanded velocity at a fixed cycle frequency. This suppresses the speed variation that gliding produces and leaves the temporal allocation unaddressed.

In this paper, we propose a unified skating-navigation policy for passive-wheel quadrupedal robots, called SkateNav. In particular, we propose two components: a) a goal-conditioned formulation that conditions the policy on a local waypoint and the remaining time, constraining where the robot must arrive and by when but not its instantaneous speed, and b) a frequency action that lets the policy advance the alternating skating cycle at a rate it determines online. Consequently, steering, propulsion, and the temporal allocation between them are decided together by a single policy. As verified across six benchmark environments and on hardware, our approach attains the lowest cost of transport in every environment. No constant cycle frequency reproduces this efficiency.

Method

A goal-conditioned policy over an adaptive skating cycle

Task formulation · skating cycle · frequency action · architecture

Why a velocity command holds a passive wheel back

Legged and actively wheeled systems can continuously generate propulsion through direct ground interaction. A passive-wheel system cannot: it relies on intermittent leg-driven pushes followed by gliding on the resulting momentum, so its motion inherently involves repeated acceleration and deceleration. A velocity-tracking objective counts exactly that variation as tracking error and suppresses it. A goal-reaching objective instead specifies where the robot should reach within a given time budget, leaving its instantaneous velocity unconstrained.

Task and command

The task is navigating a passive-wheel quadruped through a sequence of waypoints, each to be reached within a prescribed time budget. The navigation command is ct = [GBcur, GBnxt, τt] — the current and subsequent waypoints in the body frame, together with the normalized remaining time τt ∈ [0, 1] for the current waypoint. The subsequent waypoint provides route look-ahead and coincides with the current one at the final goal. Local obstacles remain unknown a priori, so the policy must adapt its skating behavior and reactively avoid obstacles under nonholonomic rolling constraints.

The skating cycle

Human skating alternates the roles of the left and right legs: while one pushes against the ground for propulsion, the other maintains rolling contact and glides. To realize this on a quadruped, the front and rear legs on each side are synchronized and a half-cycle phase offset is imposed between the left and right pairs (δ = [0, 0.5, 0, 0.5]). Each leg then follows three sequential stages — Glide (ρG = 0.55), Push (ρP = 0.15), and Recovery (ρR = 0.30). During Glide only the passive wheel remains in contact to exploit momentum, Push uses foot-ground contact for propulsion, and Recovery lifts the leg for the subsequent stroke.

The frequency action

With a fixed skating frequency, the predefined phase fractions impose a fixed temporal allocation among Glide, Push, and Recovery regardless of context. Such fixed timing cannot accommodate varying navigation demands — more frequent Push stages to establish foot-ground contact for rapid steering around obstacles, or prolonged Glide stages when sufficient momentum is available. SkateNav therefore lets the policy output a frequency action af,t at every control step, mapped to the skating frequency as fphase,t = clip(fnom + sf af,t, 0.0, 3.0) with fnom = 1.5 Hz and sf = 0.25. This preserves the stage sequence and the inter-leg offsets while allowing the duration of each stage to adapt online.

Architecture and training

The policy uses a concurrent learning framework with an asymmetric actor–critic architecture for robust sim-to-real transfer, and comprises a terrain encoder, a contact estimator, and a backbone. Its observation is a 5-tuple: a 21 × 21 local elevation map at 0.1 m resolution encoded to a 32-dimensional latent; the robot state including joint states, foot and wheel positions, body orientation and angular velocity, and the previous action; a skating-cycle observation encoding each leg's phase as sin/cos pairs; a contact state estimated from a five-step proprioceptive history; and the navigation command. The backbone outputs a = [Δq, af] ∈ ℝ13, tracked by PD controllers. The critic additionally receives the privileged domain-randomization parameters.

Navigation performance

SkateNav attains the lowest CoT in every environment and the highest success rate in all but the downhill terrain, where CEM completes more trials at a higher transport cost. The energy efficiency of the velocity-command baselines saturates regardless of tuning: varying the cost profile of MPPI and CEM shifts their success rate substantially, yet their CoT stays in a narrow band above ours. The cause lies in the objective imposed on the low-level controller — while a commanded velocity is tracked, the speed variation of a push–glide cycle counts as tracking error and is suppressed, leaving little room to coast on accumulated momentum.

No constant frequency reproduces it

A natural question is whether a better constant frequency would suffice. Sweeping the fixed clock across the range the policy can command yields a shallow CoT basin: slow clocks cannot accumulate the momentum needed to reach the waypoint in time, and the slowest setting collapses the success rate outright, while fast clocks spend energy on strokes the robot does not need. SkateNav lies below the entire curve, including its minimum, at a mean frequency inside the swept range. The gain therefore comes from redistributing pushing and gliding within an episode, not from a different constant rate.

What each component contributes

Adaptive frequency. The CoT reduction over the fixed clock grows with the propulsive demand of the terrain, from 16% on flat ground to 31% uphill.

The Glide reward. Removing it raises the consumed power in every stage pair, and the increase is largest in Glide–Glide, precisely where no propulsion is intended. The wheels are then driven against the body motion while in contact, so the intervals reserved for coasting dissipate energy instead of preserving momentum. The variant keeps the skating structure and a comparable success rate yet trails in CoT.

The skating cycle itself. Removing it leaves the largest gap — CoT rises by 42% on flat ground and 93% uphill. Its success rate nonetheless matches the other variants, because the policy converges to sustained ground contact instead of establishing distinct glide intervals. Continuous contact keeps steering available at all times, but forfeits the momentum that makes passive wheels efficient. The phase-based cycle is therefore a prerequisite for securing glide intervals, and adaptive frequency allocates their duration on top of it.

The trade-off. The momentum that gliding accumulates becomes a liability wherever it must be shed. Under nonholonomic rolling constraints, retained momentum limits the steering authority available within a given distance and makes the terminal stopping condition harder to satisfy. SkateNav accordingly trails the fixed-frequency variant in success rate on BARN-hard, while its transport cost is lower by a far larger margin. This trade-off is intrinsic to passive-wheel locomotion, since efficiency and maneuverability are coupled through the same momentum.

Metrics

Success rate (SR) is the proportion of trials in which the robot arrives within 0.2 m of the target. Cost of transport (CoT) normalizes an accumulated actuator energy proxy by the robot weight and the traveled distance, using a simplified DC motor model in which the electrical power of an actuator is the sum of mechanical power and Joule loss. Regenerative recovery is not modeled. The motor constants are those of the GIM8108-6 actuator used in our system (R = 0.73 Ω, Kt = 1.22 N·m/A). Time is the inference time per decision step on an RTX 4090 in a single-robot setting. The BARN environments are sorted by distance to the closest obstacle and grouped into three tiers; all methods share the same 1000 evaluation episodes per environment.

Real-World Deployment

Transferred to hardware without further tuning

Four indoor and outdoor sites unseen during training

We deployed the policy trained in simulation on the physical platform without further tuning. State estimation and the local elevation map are produced onboard at 10 Hz, and the policy runs at 50 Hz. Among the indoor deployments, the traverse below covers the widest range of conditions: an extended route with curves, a descending ramp, and cluttered rooms. The velocity along the trajectory is not uniform — the robot slowed through the curves and the cluttered sections, and reached its highest velocity on the descent. This matched the behavior the adaptive skating cycle produces in simulation.

Hardware

A detachable passive-wheel module on a Mini Cheetah

The foot itself serves as the non-rolling contact

Human inline skaters exploit the anisotropic rolling constraint of the skate, orienting it relative to the heading direction and pushing laterally to convert the ground reaction force into forward propulsion. Reproducing this on a quadruped requires independent control of the end-effector orientation, but the three actuated degrees of freedom per leg only control its position. Prior work therefore introduced a separate non-rolling contact feature alongside the passive wheel. Instead of adding such an element, we use the existing foot as the non-rolling contact and attach a detachable passive-wheel module: foot-ground contact generates propulsion, whereas wheel-ground contact provides low-resistance rolling.
